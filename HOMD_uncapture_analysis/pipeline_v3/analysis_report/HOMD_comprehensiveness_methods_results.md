# Methods

## Coverage of the global oral ASV catalogue by HOMD

We assembled oral 16S rRNA amplicon data from 334 publicly available BioProjects, comprising 116,099 samples from 52,298 subjects and covering all six continents and 16 amplicon-region configurations, most often V3–V4 (155 BioProjects) and V4 (104). For each BioProject, amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were obtained from the published DADA2 tables and pooled into a single cross-study catalogue of 3,396,776 ASVs, which were collapsed by exact sequence identity into 2,844,174 non-redundant ASVs. Each non-redundant ASV was aligned to the HOMD 16S rRNA RefSeq database (v16.03) with vsearch, retaining the best hit per query, and identity was computed over the full length of the query.

Non-redundant ASVs were screened for chimeras against a combined HOMD, Greengenes2 and SILVA 138.2 reference set; 744,715 sequences (26.2%) were removed. Sequences derived from host mitochondria or chloroplasts were identified by comparison with the Greengenes2 organelle references and excluded. Contamination was addressed using the extraction and sequencing controls available for a subset of BioProjects: control samples were screened for authenticity and used to build a catalogue of contaminant ASVs, and any ASV that clustered at ≥97% identity with a control-derived ASV, or that was assigned to a genus repeatedly recovered from controls or previously reported as a reagent contaminant, was flagged and excluded.

An ASV was considered represented by HOMD only if its best HOMD hit reached 97% identity — the conventional species-level boundary for 16S rRNA — over at least 99% of the query length; the latter condition accounts for the fact that HOMD holds near-full-length reference sequences whereas the query ASVs are shorter fragments. For the 1,871,022 ASVs that remained after removal of chimeric, contamination-flagged and organelle-flagged sequences, we computed the cumulative fraction of ASVs, of reads and of subject-level detections with best-hit identity ≥ x, for x from 0 to 100% in 0.1% steps; a subject-level detection was defined as the presence of an ASV in one subject. The per-phylum curves were computed on the 1,833,261 of these ASVs whose best HOMD hit could be assigned to a phylum; the remaining 37,761 had no HOMD hit and are therefore counted only in the combined curve. Coverage was summarised overall, per phylum and per HOMD species. To distinguish species with systematically incomplete HOMD representation from those with only occasional mismatches, we classified the 801 HOMD species to which at least one ASV mapped by the proportion of their ASVs below 97% identity (<50% vs ≥50%) and by ASV richness (<5,000 vs ≥5,000 ASVs).

## Recurrent oral ASVs not represented by HOMD

Because recurrent sequences are unlikely to be technical artefacts, we used cross-study recurrence to identify ASVs whose absence from HOMD is likely to be biologically meaningful rather than sporadic. ASVs detected in at least two BioProjects and supported by at least 50 reads in total (n = 54,277) were inserted into the Greengenes2 2022.10 reference tree by fragment insertion (SEPP). Placed ASVs and reference tips were then clustered with TreeCluster using a maximum clade distance of 0.03, approximately equivalent to 97% identity, which groups the sequences into OTU-like clusters and links each ASV to its closest described relatives. An ASV was annotated to the nearest reference tip when one fell within the species boundary; otherwise it was assigned the lowest common ancestor of the reference tips in its cluster, and ASVs whose clusters contained no reference tip were annotated no deeper than genus. HOMD species names were reconciled with Greengenes2/GTDB nomenclature so that species renamed in Greengenes2 were not counted as missing from HOMD. A cluster was treated as represented by HOMD if at least one of its members could be assigned at species level to a species present in HOMD, and as not represented otherwise. To retain only organisms with consistent geographical support, we required clusters to be detected in more than 50 subjects, in at least two BioProjects and on at least two continents, and excluded clusters containing contamination-flagged or organelle-flagged sequences; 486 clusters met all criteria.

# Results

## Most ASV diversity in global oral samples falls below the HOMD species boundary

Among the 1,871,022 non-redundant ASVs that remained after filtering, 84.1% reached at least 70% identity to eHOMD, but only 51.6% reached the 97% species-level boundary and only 9.8% were identical to an eHOMD reference. The picture changed markedly when ASVs were weighted by how often they were observed: identity thresholds of 90%, 97% and 100% accounted for 97.0%, 95.0% and 76.2% of all reads, and for 88.6%, 83.4% and 53.6% of all subject-level detections, respectively (Fig. 1B). The ASV diversity that HOMD does not represent is therefore largely the rare tail of the community: it dominates counts of distinct sequences but contributes comparatively little to the reads and subject-level signal on which oral community profiles rest.

## Completeness varies sharply among phyla and is not explained by phylum abundance

The proportion of ASVs reaching 97% identity ranged from 73.5% in Fusobacteriota and 67.3% in Bacteroidota — the phyla best represented in HOMD — to 0.1% in Chlamydiota and Cyanobacteriota, and bore no simple relationship to how many ASVs or how many HOMD reference sequences a phylum contains; Bacillota, the most ASV-rich phylum, reached only 48.1% (Fig. 1B). The 607,742 ASVs with identity between 70% and 97% were dominated by a small number of phyla: Bacillota alone contributed 43.0%, and Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota and Actinomycetota together accounted for 90.1%. This composition differed sharply from that of the 965,397 ASVs above 97% identity, in which Bacillota (31.9%) and Bacteroidota (27.3%) dominated and Pseudomonadota contributed only 16.0%. The phyla that dominate the poorly matched fraction are thus not simply those that dominate the well-matched fraction, and phylum-level completeness cannot be inferred from the size of a phylum in the oral community.

## Species with incomplete HOMD representation are numerous and collectively account for a large share of ASVs

Classifying the 801 HOMD species to which at least one ASV mapped by coverage gap and ASV richness separated species with largely complete HOMD representation from those with systematic gaps (Fig. 1C). Twenty-seven species combined high ASV richness with a high coverage gap, 59 combined high richness with a low gap, 348 low richness with a high gap, and 367 low richness with a low gap; these groups accounted for 11.9%, 23.1%, 29.9% and 35.1% of all ASVs, respectively. Overall, 41.8% of ASVs mapped to species for which most ASVs fell below the 97% boundary, and this gap was not confined to a few abundant species: the 348 low-richness/high-gap species contributed more ASVs (29.9%) than the 27 high-richness/high-gap species (11.9%), even though the latter alone represented 218,553 ASVs.

The species highlighted within each group illustrate distinct biological patterns. The most ASV-rich species in the high-richness/high-gap group included the gut-associated commensals *Agathobacter rectalis* and *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* as well as *Hominimerdicola aceti*, all with median HOMD identities of only 88–92% despite thousands of oral ASVs; the group also contained *Arthrospira platensis*, an environmental cyanobacterium with 9,104 ASVs of which 99.9% fell below 97% identity, indicating that non-oral or environmental sequence sources remain an important contributor to low-identity ASVs. In contrast, the most ASV-rich species in the high-richness/low-gap group were canonical oral commensals and opportunists — *Haemophilus parainfluenzae*, *Streptococcus mitis* and *Porphyromonas pasteri* — all with median identities of at least 99%. Among low-richness/high-gap species, those with the fewest ASVs (*Fusobacterium* HMT-205, *Fusobacterium simiae* and *Schaalia* HMT-240) were mostly species with few HOMD reference sequences and low median identities (49–91%), whereas two well-known clinical species with many reference sequences — *Haemophilus influenzae* and *Staphylococcus aureus* — still had more than half of their ASVs below 97% identity. The low-richness/low-gap species with the fewest ASVs (e.g. *Staphylococcus saccharolyticus*, *Gardnerella leopoldii* and *Neisseria perflava*) were rare but well covered, with median identities of 98–100%. HOMD coverage is therefore high for classical oral commensals, whereas intraspecies diversity, non-oral or environmental relatives and poorly characterised HOMD species contribute recurrently to the observed identity gap.

## Recurrent, geographically widespread oral ASVs remain unrepresented by HOMD

Of the 486 clusters that were detected in more than 50 subjects, in at least two BioProjects and on at least two continents, 421 (86.6%) could not be assigned to any HOMD species. Among the 43 multi-member clusters shown in Fig. 1D, 39 were unrepresented in HOMD; the same was true of 35 of the 40 most prevalent singleton ASVs (Fig. S1). Multi-member clusters contained up to 112 ASVs (median 4), reached subject prevalences up to 3,427, and were recovered from up to 216 BioProjects and all six continents, while the 40 most prevalent singleton ASVs were each detected in 343–1,531 subjects. These organisms therefore recur across populations, sequencing regions and oral sites rather than reflecting cohort-specific or technical artefacts. Their closest HOMD matches nevertheless lay below the species boundary (median 87% identity, range 42–97%), and their distances to the nearest reference tip consistently exceeded the species boundary, showing that biologically recurrent components of the global oral microbiome are still absent from the HOMD reference catalogue (Fig. 1D).

# Figure legend

**Figure 1. Representation of global oral microbiome ASV diversity by the eHOMD reference catalogue.**

**(A) Assembly of the oral ASV catalogue.** Oral 16S rRNA amplicon data were assembled from 334 publicly available BioProjects, comprising 116,099 samples from 52,298 subjects. Per-BioProject DADA2 tables contributed 3,396,776 ASVs, which were collapsed by exact sequence identity into 2,844,174 non-redundant ASVs. Each non-redundant ASV was compared with eHOMD v16.03, retaining the best hit per ASV, and the catalogue was then filtered to remove chimeric, chloroplast- and mitochondrion-derived, and control-derived contaminant sequences. Panels (B)–(D) are based on the 1,871,022 ASVs that survived filtering; the per-phylum curves in (B) use the 1,833,261 of these whose best eHOMD hit could be assigned to a phylum, the remaining 37,761 having no eHOMD hit at all.

**(B) Identity of oral ASVs to eHOMD, by phylum and by metric.** The upper left plot shows, for the whole catalogue, the cumulative distribution of best-hit identity to eHOMD under the three weightings — ASV counts, reads and subject-level prevalence — drawn over the identity range ≥70%: at any identity x on the x axis the curve gives the proportion whose best hit reaches at least x. The dashed line marks the 97% species-level boundary, where the three curves stand at 51.6%, 95.0% and 83.4%, respectively. The upper right plot gives the same quantity per phylum at the 97% boundary, with the phyla ordered by the ASV-weighted proportion and the three points of each phylum joined, so the ranking under one weighting can be read against the other two; the proportion ranges from *Fusobacteriota* (73.5%) to *Chlamydiota* and *Cyanobacteriota* (0.1%). The lower plot shows the phylum composition of each identity band — ASVs below the 97% boundary and ASVs at or above it — under each weighting, as 100% stacked bars; segments are held in the same order in every bar so that the bars can be read straight across, and those above 5% are labelled with their percentage. The poorly matched band is dominated by Bacillota (43.0%), Pseudomonadota (24.0%), Bacteroidota (14.6%) and Actinomycetota (8.5%), whereas the well-matched band is dominated by Bacillota (31.9%) and Bacteroidota (27.3%).

**(C) Species-level coverage gap versus ASV richness.** Each point is one of the 801 eHOMD species to which at least one ASV mapped. The x axis gives the proportion of that species' ASVs whose best eHOMD identity falls below 97%, i.e. the fraction of its ASV diversity that eHOMD does not represent at species level; the y axis gives the number of non-redundant ASVs mapped to the species. Point size indicates the number of eHOMD reference sequences for that species, and point colour the group to which the species belongs. Dashed lines at 50% and 5,000 ASVs divide the species into four groups; the inset boxes give the number of species and the share of all ASVs in each group (high richness/high gap: 27 species, 11.9% of ASVs; high richness/low gap: 59 species, 23.1%; low richness/high gap: 348 species, 29.9%; low richness/low gap: 367 species, 35.1%). The diagram above the panel shows how the two coordinates are obtained for a single species: its ASVs are stacked into those whose best hit falls below 97% identity (a) and those at or above it (b), so that the bar height is y = a + b and the fraction of the bar taken by a is x = a / (a + b). Labelled points are the three most ASV-rich species in each of the two high-richness groups and the three least ASV-rich species in each of the two low-richness groups, plus the two species with the most eHOMD reference sequences in the low-richness/high-gap group.

**(D) Recurrent oral clusters that are not represented by eHOMD.** The panel shows the 43 multi-member clusters, out of the 486 clusters detected in more than 50 subjects, in at least two BioProjects and on at least two continents, that contain more than one ASV; one row is shown per cluster, labelled with its species- or genus-level annotation, and rows are sorted by subject prevalence. Colour tracks, from left to right, give: the tree distance from the cluster's ASV to its nearest annotated reference tip (distance to known tips); the number of ASVs in the cluster; the number of BioProjects; the number of continents in which it was detected; the best-hit identity to eHOMD; whether the species-level annotation is present in eHOMD; and the oral sites at which it was detected, with multiple sites per row shown as separate tiles. The rightmost panel gives prevalence as a boxplot, with the individual member ASVs of each cluster overlaid. Within the "Taxonomy in eHOMD" track, blue marks annotations that are present in eHOMD and pale grey marks those that are absent from it. **Fig. S1** shows the 40 singleton ASVs with the highest subject prevalence in the same layout, with prevalence given as a bar chart.
